Abstract
Momentum is central to stochastic optimization in deep learning, including the training of large language models.
Classical momentum methods typically rely on a fast exponential moving average (EMA) scheme, which discounts
past gradients on a fixed timescale and may underweight older gradients that remain informative. This creates a
trade-off between retaining long-term history and adapting to recent changes. We introduce Hybrid Momentum
(HyM), which combines a linearly weighted average of past gradients with a geometrically discounted fast momentum.
The former assigns weights proportional to the gradients’ iteration indices and retains a growing span of history,
while the latter emphasizes recent gradients. This hybrid construction is modular and can be combined with adaptive
preconditioning and other update transformations. In particular, we propose HyMUON, which applies Newton–Schulz
orthogonalization to the hybrid direction. Illustrative examples and controlled experiments demonstrate the benefits of
combining gradient information across these two timescales. Across image classification, machine translation, and
language modeling, HyM and HyMUON match or improve upon strong baselines. HyMUON improves over Muon on
IWSLT14, and in both the 100k- and 600k-step GPT-2 124M OpenWebText regimes. For LLaMA-1.3B pretraining on
C4, HyMUON achieves a final validation loss of 2.777, compared with 2.903 for Muon.

Introduction

Momentum methods improve stochastic optimization by combining the current gradient with information from earlier
updates. In deep learning, this history is usually maintained through exponential moving averaging (EMA), which
assigns geometrically decaying weights to past gradients, as in Heavy-Ball momentum and commonly used Nesterov
updates [Polyak, 1964, Sutskever et al., 2013]. EMA emphasizes recent gradients while strongly reducing the influence
of older gradients on subsequent updates. When gradient contributions that reduce a particular target loss occur
intermittently across mini-batches, retaining a longer history may help accumulate these contributions.
By combining momentum with iterate averaging, Defazio et al. [2024] proposed the Schedule-Free method, which
achieves strong empirical performance without a prescribed schedule for learning rate decay. The empirical success of
Schedule-Free optimization motivates revisiting how this history is accumulated. To see how its averaging mechanism
retains past gradients, consider the Schedule-Free SGD recurrence with a constant step size
