Abstract
Muon updates matrix-valued parameters along approximately orthogonalized directions and often
trains large language models faster than AdamW, yet it remains unclear when and why orthogonalization
helps. We study PolarGD, the orthogonalized update at the core of Muon, on matrix quadratics, where
optimizers can be analyzed in closed form, and compare update directions under line search to remove
the effect of learning-rate tuning. Orthogonalization pays off when the gradient is far from low rank and
its largest singular directions see high curvature. PolarGD reaches the minimizer in one step whenever
the rows of the error are orthogonal with equal norms, for any Hessian, whereas gradient descent needs
all Hessian eigenvalues to be equal; and unlike sign descent, PolarGD is unaffected by rotations of the
problem. Over many steps, PolarGD acts as gradient descent preconditioned by the current iterate, so its
speed is governed by the conditioning of the iterate rather than of the Hessian. This explains fast regimes
as well as a slow one, caused by tiny singular values of the iterate, in which PolarGD is much slower
than gradient descent. Finally, the few Newton–Schulz iterations used in Muon treat these tiny singular
values as gradient descent does, so fewer iterations can converge faster than exact orthogonalization; in a
transformer trained with Muon, about a quarter of the singular values of the attention matrices fall in
this regime.

Introduction

Muon (Jordan et al., 2024b) has emerged as a strong optimizer for training large neural networks. It has
matched or outperformed AdamW in language-model pretraining, from speedrun benchmarks (Jordan et al.,
2024a) to large-scale training (Liu et al., 2025; Shah et al., 2025), and standard optimizers more broadly in
vision (Jordan et al., 2024b; Pethick et al., 2025), diffusion (Schaipp, 2025), and tabular models (Gorishniy
et al., 2026). For two-dimensional parameter blocks, Muon approximately orthogonalizes the momentum
matrix with a few Newton–Schulz iterations, that is, it steps along an approximation of the polar factor
polar(G) = U V ⊤ of G = U ΣG V ⊤ . The same direction is shared by a broader family of matrix optimizers. It is
the steepest-descent direction for the spectral norm, used in stochastic spectral descent (Carlson et al., 2015a,
2016, 2015b) and revisited by Bernstein and Newhouse (2024). It is also the linear-minimization-oracle (LMO)
direction of norm-constrained methods (Pethick et al., 2025), and Shampoo (Gupta et al., 2018) reduces to it
when preconditioner accumulation is switched off (Bernstein and Newhouse, 2024). Controlled benchmarks
